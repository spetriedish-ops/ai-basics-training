So how do we work with the fact that the agent doesn’t remember us but we’re filling up the context window getting it up to speed? as recently as this past spring, when token-matching was encouraged, AI-pilled folks were putting together elaborate file systems commonly referred to as Personal Context Portfolios. These often contained a myriad of files covering different topics, like one text document (markdown file) that lists all the people you collaborate with often and their roles iin relation to yours. Another file might contain details about you, your role, and what you care about. These portfolios added up to represent a decent snapshot of the bare minimum an agent would need to know about you to be useful. At the beginning of each new session in which you were planning on doing real work and not just asking a one-off question, you would point your agent at the group of files so it could learn about you without you having to retype everything yourself. Maintaining these files also let you switch back and forth between different vendor’s agents. So you could point Claude and ChatGPT at the same Personal Context Portfolio without having to maintain separate file systems. But this wasn’t an ideal long term solution for a couple reasons. First, Consuming all those files at the beginning of the session still filled the context window. And second, you are now in a position where you have to keep these files up to date in order for your agents to remain useful. If the fiscal year turns over and your accounts and supporting team change, you may find yourself starting from scratch. Now, mature users of AI agents know that you’re best off letting your agent edit markdown files for you as you work together. That way, each time you share a new piece of information with the agent, you know it will be able to carry that information forward and your other agents looking at the same files will inherit those updates.

But we still haven’t solved the issue of the context window filling up. It’s also not realistic for every single thing you mention to your agent to live in your Personal Context Portfolio. So you’re not quite at Dory levels of amnesia yet, but it’s still like having a teammate who remembers general things about you but with each new conversation, you have to reexplain things you have talked to it about before. Frustrating, and you burn through additional tokens doing it. Sub-optimal.

Another approach is the mono-thread. When a lot of people start working with an agent and begin to feel the pain of losing all their context when they start a new session, they begin to do ALL of their work in the same session or thread. The more you work in that session across all your work areas, the better the agent is at understanding your preferences. You’ll find that the output gets progressively better and better. There are a couple of reasons why this approach isn’t ideal. The first is pretty obvious given how much we’ve been beating the drum on this. The more context in your context window, the more expensive each interaction becomes. This was less of a problem back in the token maxing era before token budgets were implemented and back when leadership looked at token consumption as an positive indicator that you were adopting AI. However, it still had a second critical weakness.

What happens when the room gets really crowded with context? First and not unlike a human, the agent is more likely to have a hard time finding the right information in the sea of information. You may remember this from the MCP discussion. This is exacerbated by the second. Every context window has limits, and as those limits are approached, compaction occurs (sometimes called “pruning”). Compaction is another pretty well named function. Think of the trash compactor scene from Star Wars episode IV, a New Hope. Trash is squished together to make room for more trash. All the original trash is still present, but it’s been condensed down into a summary that takes up significantly less space. This opens up a bunch of space in the context widow, but important details and even instructions can sometimes be lost during the compaction process. And the worst part is, you don’t necessarily know what’s been lost, unless you check in with your agent and ask if it remembers something specific. There was a viral article earlier in 2026 about an employee at Meta who had their agent perform some clean up on their personal inbox. The agent was instructed to not delete anything without first validating with the user. The agent did a great job, so she thought she would have it take a look at her work inbox which was filled with over 3k emails. As the agent worked, it’s context window filled up and at some point during compaction, it lost the instructions to not delete anything. The next thing the Meta employee knew, her agent had wiped out her entire inbox.

Thankfully, the AI world moves fast, and it didn’t take long for the labs to make enhancements to their harnesses like increased context windows, improved compaction techniques, and implementing persistent memory. If you’ve used an agent through Anthropic or OAI’s harnesses in the past 6 months, you’ve probably noticed that it does remember information about you across sessions.
